📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do you earn the right to say whether a work of art is any good?
In Lesson 1.01 you learned the vocabulary of looking. Now you will learn the discipline of responding: a four-step method critics use so that judgment comes last, after careful looking, instead of first.

I will be able to distinguish the questions of art history from the questions of art criticism.
2
I will be able to name and order the four steps of the DAIJ method.
3
I will be able to apply DAIJ to a single artwork in my own words.

📚 PART 1 · TWO WAYS OF SEEING~10 MIN

Imagine two people standing in front of the same painting. The first asks: Who painted this, and when? What was happening in the world that made someone want to paint it? What earlier art is it answering? That is the art historian. The second asks: What is going on in this picture? How is it built? What is it trying to make me feel or think, and does it succeed? That is the art critic. Same painting, two jobs.

The two depend on each other. A critic who knows nothing of a work’s history can misread it; a historian who cannot describe what is actually on the canvas is only doing paperwork. This course trains you in both, and they meet in one discipline: you describe before you explain, and explain before you judge.

ART HISTORY asks…
Who? When? Where? For whom? Why then? What came before it, and what did it change?
ART CRITICISM asks…
What do I see? How is it organized? What does it mean? Is it successful, and on what evidence?
📚 PART 2 · THE DAIJ METHOD~18 MIN

Critics need a method so that snap judgments (“I like it,” “that’s ugly”) do not arrive before any looking has happened. The classic method has four steps, in this order. The order is the whole point: you earn your judgment by first proving you looked.

D
Describe. What do you see? List only what is literally there: the subject, the figures, the objects, the setting. No opinions yet, no meanings. Just the facts a camera would catch.
A
Analyze. How is the work organized? Name the two or three Elements of Art and Principles of Design that are really driving it, and say how. This is where Lesson 1.01’s vocabulary goes to work.
I
Interpret. What is the artist trying to say? Now you offer meaning, mood, or message, but every claim must point back to something in your Describe and Analyze steps. Interpretation without evidence is just guessing.
J
Judge. Does the work succeed at what it set out to do, and how do you know? Your judgment rests on everything above it. This is the last step, never the first.

🔍 ARTWORK SPOTLIGHT · DAIJ (worked example)
Oath of the Horatii, Jacques-Louis David (1784)
Musée du Louvre, Paris
Describe: Three young men stretch their arms toward three swords held up by an older man at the center. Behind him, women and children sink together in grief. The setting is a plain stone courtyard with three round arches.
Analyze: David splits the picture in two. The men are all straight lines, rigid arms and legs, while the women are soft curves, creating sharp contrast. Each group sits under its own arch, and the swords fall at the exact center, so emphasis lands on the oath itself.
Interpret: The hard, straight men and the collapsing women suggest a conflict between public duty and private love. David seems to argue that loyalty to country can demand a terrible cost at home.
Judge: It succeeds. Every choice, the splitting line, the contrast of bodies, the centered swords, drives the one idea home, and the painting is impossible to misread.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI & INTERPRETATION
An AI chatbot will happily produce a full DAIJ paragraph in seconds, and it will sound confident. But it sometimes invents details that are not in the painting and meanings no scholar holds, because it predicts likely-sounding text rather than looking at the actual image in front of you. It cannot verify its own claims against the work, and on this assignment, that verifying (pointing to what is really there) is exactly the skill being graded. Use your own eyes first.
📖 VOCABULARY FROM THIS LESSON
DAIJThe four-step critique method: Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Judge, in that order.
DescribeNaming only what is literally in the work, with no meaning or opinion.
AnalyzeNaming how the work is organized through its Elements and Principles.
InterpretProposing what the work means, with every claim tied to visual evidence.
JudgeDeciding whether the work succeeds, based on the three steps before it.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

DAIJ is the spine of this entire course. Every analysis, every comparison, every museum journal you write this year will move through these four steps. Holding back your judgment until you have truly looked is a kind of patience and honesty, and it is the habit this method is here to build. Master the discipline now, with two clear paintings, and you will be ready when the works get stranger, older, and harder to read.
