📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have looked at 30,000 years of art. Now the choices are yours: which works deserve a wall, and what story will you tell with them?
For your Semester 1 project you become the curator. You will select 5 to 7 works from Units 1 to 6, organize them around a theme, and present them in a digital exhibit with your own labels and interpretation. This guide shows you how; the assignment that follows is where you build it.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT A CURATOR ACTUALLY DOES~15 MIN
👀 IMAGINE THE GALLERY
Picture walking into a museum room. The works on the walls were not gathered at random. Someone chose them, arranged their order, and wrote the small labels beside each one. That someone is a curator, and an exhibit is their argument made visible.

A museum exhibit is not just a pile of famous things. It is a curated argument: a curator picks works that speak to one another, puts them in a meaningful order, and uses labels to help visitors see what they might miss. Your job is the same. The theme you choose is your idea; the works are your evidence; the labels are your voice.

A strong theme could cut across periods. For example: Art and the Sacred (a Lascaux painting, a ziggurat, Hagia Sophia, a Gothic cathedral), or The Human Figure Across Time (the Venus of Willendorf, the kouros, the Nike, the Augustus), or Power Made Visible (the Palette of Narmer, the Colosseum, Trajan’s Column). The best themes let you compare and connect, not just list.

🏛️ CURATOR IS A REAL JOB · CAREERS IN THE WORLD OF ART
The skills you are practicing in this project are practiced for a living. A curator selects and interprets works for exhibits. A conservator is part scientist, part artist, repairing and preserving works so they survive. A registrar tracks every object a museum owns and moves. An art historian researches, teaches, and writes about art. An appraiser judges authenticity and value for sales and insurance. An art educator teaches others to see. These careers usually ask for a degree in art history, studio art, or museum studies. The same love of art also lives outside a paycheck: volunteer docents give museum tours, collectors build small collections, and lifelong museum-goers simply keep looking. Career or avocation, the shared requirement is the skill this course builds: an eye that can describe, analyze, interpret, and judge.
📚 PART 2 · CHOOSING YOUR WORKS~15 MIN

Pick 5 to 7 works from Units 1 to 6. Aim for range: draw from at least three different units so your exhibit spans time and culture, not just one period. Choose works you can say something real about, using the analyses you already wrote this semester. Every work must be one we actually studied or that appears in the course Artwork Image Banks, so you can cite it accurately.

📚 PART 3 · WRITING A MUSEUM LABEL~20 MIN

Each work gets a label: a short block of facts, then a few sentences of your analysis. The facts identify the work; the analysis is where you, the curator, help the visitor see. Build your label’s analysis straight from four-step analysis: a line that describes, a line that analyzes the Elements or Principles at work, and a line that interprets the meaning and says why it belongs in your exhibit.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI & THE CURATOR’S JUDGMENT
An AI tool can draft a tidy label or suggest a theme in seconds. But two things remain yours: choosing which works are worth showing and why they speak to each other, and verifying that every fact (title, artist, date) is correct, since AI sometimes invents them. The exhibit is graded on your curatorial judgment and your accuracy. Use your own analyses from this semester, and check every fact against the course materials.
📚 PART 4 · BUILDING & PRESENTING IT~10 MIN

Build your exhibit as a PowerPoint presentation, your digital gallery. A clear structure:

• Title slide: your exhibit’s name, your name, and a one-line description of the theme.
• Introduction slide: a short paragraph stating your theme and why it matters.
• One slide per work (5 to 7 total): the image, the identifying facts, and your label analysis.
• Closing reflection slide: what your exhibit reveals when the works are seen together.
• Sources slide: a cited list of every image and any sources you used.
🧭 OPTIONAL · PRESENT IT IN VR
If you would like, you may also stage your exhibit in the course virtual environment: place your chosen images on the walls of a gallery space and add a sticky-note label beside each, then capture a short walkthrough. This is an optional enrichment; the PowerPoint is the required deliverable.
